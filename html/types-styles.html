What Makes Each Style Unique

Alpine Climbing

The classic discipline — a blend of rock, snow, and ice in high mountain environments. Alpine climbing demands versatility, route-finding skills, and self-sufficiency.

  • Mountaineering on glaciated peaks
  • Requires rope work, glacier travel, weather reading
  • Example: Mont Blanc, Matterhorn, Eiger

Sport Climbing

Climbing on pre-bolted routes with a focus on athletic difficulty and movement. The safest and most accessible form of rock climbing for beginners.

  • Fixed bolts for protection
  • Emphasis on technique, strength, and endurance
  • Example: Kalymnos, Ceüse, Yosemite sport crags

Traditional (Trad) Climbing

The purest form of rock climbing — placing your own protective gear as you ascend. Trad climbers must master judgment, gear placement, and route-reading.

  • Cams, nuts, hexes for protection
  • Multi-pitch routes and big walls
  • Example: Yosemite, Squamish, Chamonix granite

Ice Climbing

Ascending frozen waterfalls, gullies, and ice faces using crampons and ice tools. A technical, seasonal discipline requiring precision and courage.

  • Waterfall ice (WI) & alpine ice (AI)
  • Requires sharp tools, crampons, screws
  • Example: Ouray, Cogne, Canadian Rockies

Mixed Climbing

The intersection of rock and ice — climbing routes that alternate between dry rock and frozen surfaces. Mixed climbing is the most technical discipline.

  • Dry tooling on rock with ice axes
  • M-grades for mixed routes
  • Example: Helmcken Falls, Eptam wall

Expedition Climbing

The ultimate test — multi-week ascents of the world's highest mountains. Expedition climbing requires logistical planning, teamwork, and extreme endurance.

  • High-altitude peaks above 7000m
  • Oxygen, porters, base camps
  • Example: Everest, K2, Denali, Aconcagua
